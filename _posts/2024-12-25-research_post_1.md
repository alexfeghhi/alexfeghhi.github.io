<h1>Intro</h1>

Every couple decades, there's a new framework that will solve AGI(Artificial General Intelligence). Historically, the first approaches focused on rule-based symbolic systems. Now, the current approach is focused on scaling up deep learning. Some say that in the future, we could combine neural networks with symbolic learning. Or maybe, we can go back to the Bayesian approach.(rewording, expand each approach into a paragraph, give a paragraph or two on scaling laws. final concluding paragraph listing future approaches and the guy diggin meme)

Neurosymbolic, Bayesian, ....

I'm not trying to cite the existence of various approaches towards developing AGI as proof that no single approach could solve AGI, but rather to hint at the underlying complexity of the problem. I believe that before delving into a particular approach, one should take time to step back and attempt to define the problem of engineering intelligence and understand the inherit difficulties in doing so.

Of course, this difficult is not obvious to everyone. Indeed, many intellectuals have thought substantial problems within AI were trivial. Marvin Minsky, Professor at MIT, once assigned a graduate student the summer problem of solving computer vision. Other notable examples include Alan Turing(), other examples...

This, of course, begs the question as to why the problem of Artifical Intelligence can be perceived to be so trivial? The intellectual justification for this lies with the Computational Theory of Mind.

<h2>Computational Theory of Mind</h2>(needs alot more research)

Those unfamiliar with computer science may not understand that behind our practical computers, there exists layers of abstract theory articulating what it means for something to be computational.

In the early 1900s, researcher came about with a variety of approaches aimed at defining exactly that. The church-turing thesis holds that the turing machines define algorithms. The thesis has been held for a variety of computational systems. The take away here is that by manipulating representations of information, we can solve problems.

The computational theory of mind attributes cognition and consciousness to information processing. Personally, I think of neurons as representing current state. Anyway, the intuitive understanding of the CTM is that there is no "ghost in the machine", just information processing.(switch order; describe computational theory of mind as what it isn't, ghost in the machine, then information processing).

The conclusion that can be drawn from this is that if human intelligence is an algorithm, we ought to be able to replicate it.

Now that we've understand why artifical intelligence may be perceivied as a trivial problem, let's understand why it is difficult. I think the key is to understand the following: although the human brain is executing an algorithm, the algoirthm may infact be thousands of algorithms hiding under a trenchcoat, which would still be considered one algorithm.

Put yourself in the shoes of someone tryna make AGI. Many of the problems required are very different. Consider (examples). This leads to (estimate of domain specific algorithms). Of course, these algoirthms do not exist in isolation. There are compression algorithms which make others work together.

Let's step away from natural intelligence. We get an sequence of frames, containing audio, visual, etc... We have to pick from an exceddingly small number of choices. The enemy here is combinatorial explosion. We have to use innate knowledge.

Well, not necessarily. If we had infinite compute, we could just make a lookup table. What I want you to do is to stop thinking in a pure utilirian, standard AI mindset. We want to think about creating intelligence itself.

Looking at what we've seen, it is a combinaton of the problem-solving requirements and computational efficiency. Consider the problem of solving a line. We can do that efficinetly. This is intelligent, but not in the way human intelligence is. A lookup table out performs humans, but is not intelligence.

Intelligence, in its human and artifical form, is a result of solving the appropriate set of problems with computational effieincy.

<h2>Evolutionary Psychology</h2>
Evo psych lets us analyze problems and how humans evolve to solve them. This is different than the AI case because we need to consider a variety of factors. This is called conceptual integration.

My criticism is that they don't get computational enough. Of course, this is not their goal. However, our goal is to use our understand of the problems humans solve to define the appropriate set of problems of all inteligence beings to solve, as well as get inspiration for potential solutions to the problems. For us, we need to get more computational and compare human approaches with potential artifical approaches.
